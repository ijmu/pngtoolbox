PNG-8 vs PNG-24 vs PNG-32: which one actually saves you space

Every PNG is one of three things: an indexed-color image with a fixed palette, full color without transparency, or full color with transparency. Most guides explain this with jargon. This one explains it with the only question that matters — which one should your file be, and how much smaller does the right choice actually make it? Measured, not asserted.

By PNGToolbox Editorial Published 2026-11-07 Last reviewed 2026-11-07 How we test

At a glance

  • "PNG-8", "PNG-24" and "PNG-32" are designer shorthand, not spec terms — the PNG specification defines color types, and none of these names appear in it.
  • Measured on our corpus: PNG-8 shrank a 4-color logo by 11% versus PNG-24 and a flat-art graphic by 15% — useful, not magical.
  • The trap in the other direction: on our gradient test image, PNG-8 came out 40% larger than PNG-24. Palette reduction is not always a win.
  • A photograph forced into PNG-8 at 256 colors was still 3.6 times the size of a JPEG at quality 85 — that is a format-choice failure, not a PNG failure.

First, the terminology problem

"PNG-8", "PNG-24" and "PNG-32" are designer shorthand. The PNG specification — published as ISO/IEC 15948 and identical to the W3C recommendation — defines color types, and the shorthand maps onto them roughly like this:

ShorthandPNG color typeColorsAlpha (transparency)
PNG-8Type 3 — indexed colorUp to 256, from a paletteYes — palette entries can carry alpha
PNG-24Type 2 — truecolor RGB16.7 million (8 bits × 3 channels)No
PNG-32Type 6 — truecolor + alpha (RGBA)16.7 millionYes, full 8-bit alpha

Two things people get wrong immediately:

  1. "PNG-32" is not an official term. The specification (and every serious tool) calls it truecolor with alpha, color type 6. The "32" just counts bits: 8 red + 8 green + 8 blue + 8 alpha. You will not find "PNG-32" anywhere in the specification — the term comes from how export dialogs in 2000s design software labeled their options.
  2. PNG-8 can have transparency too. The old rule of thumb "need transparency → use PNG-24/32" is outdated. Indexed PNGs support per-palette-entry alpha, so a PNG-8 logo with soft edges is perfectly possible.

The three modes side by side

The intuition: PNG-8 stores one palette index per pixel (a single byte or less), while PNG-24/32 store three or four bytes per pixel. For graphics with few distinct colors, storing "color #37" instead of three full channel values wins every time — if the palette itself stays small and the index stream stays regular. The measurement below shows where that logic holds and where it breaks.

Three rows deserve a second look, because they contradict the folk wisdom:

  • The logo and flat-art rows win less than expected. With only a handful of colors, PNG-8's savings are real but modest — 11 to 20%. The palette table itself costs bytes, and modern PNG filters already compress flat areas well in truecolor mode. PNG-8 is the right answer here, but it is not a tenfold win.
  • The gradient row goes the wrong way. At 1,226 distinct colors the image does not fit a 256-entry palette, so quantization adds dithering noise — and noise is exactly what PNG's filters cannot compress. PNG-8 came out 40% larger than PNG-24, while also looking worse. When an image has too many colors for the palette, indexed color loses twice.
  • The photo row wins on bytes and loses on everything else. Crushing ~1.1 million colors to 256 cut the file 79% — but the result is visibly dithered and banded. And for perspective: the same photograph encoded directly as JPEG at quality 85 weighs 183.5 KB from the same measurement run, 3.6 times smaller than the PNG-8. (Our published benchmark's 140.3 KB JPEG figure is not comparable — it re-encodes a WebP that has already smoothed the detail.) A photo saved as PNG-8 is a format-choice failure, not a PNG failure.

Source: measured on 7 November 2026 with Pillow 12.3.0 on the published corpus; raw per-format CSV at ./assets/bench/study-a-format.csv, the JPEG q85 figure from the same run.

When to use which

Choose PNG-8 when

  • The image has fewer than ~256 distinct colors: logos, icons, flat illustrations, pixel art, simple diagrams.
  • You need transparency and the edges are hard (an icon on a button), or you can accept palette-based soft edges.
  • You are delivering graphics to environments that reject WebP — see our WebP compatibility guide — and still want the smallest safe file.

Choose PNG-24 when

  • The image is photographic or has gradients, and you cannot use lossy formats (JPEG/WebP) because the pipeline demands PNG.
  • Transparency does not matter. If it does not matter, do not pay for the alpha channel.

Choose PNG-32 when

  • You need truecolor and full anti-aliased transparency: cut-out product photos, layered UI mockups, overlays.
  • Note the trap: an all-opaque image saved as PNG-32 carries an alpha channel that compresses well but still adds bytes — in our corpus run, 15 to 18% on the truecolor rows. If nothing in the file is transparent, export as PNG-24.

What this means when you export

Our converter works on the truecolor side of the family — it reads any PNG and exports JPG or WebP, and the white background tool flattens a transparent PNG-32 onto a solid colour, which produces exactly the PNG-24 case: truecolor, no alpha, smaller file, with the background baked in where the transparency used to be. What our tools deliberately do not offer yet is PNG-8 palette reduction: a good quantizer needs careful dithering choices, and a browser canvas export cannot provide the preview-and-tune loop that deciding "will 64 colors still look right?" requires. For palette export, use an editor that shows the palette and a live preview, and judge the result at 100% zoom — the measured table above tells you whether the fight is worth having: near or under 256 distinct colors, try PNG-8; well above, stay truecolor or switch to a lossy format.

Common misconceptions

  • "PNG-32 is a different, newer PNG format." It is not. It is ordinary truecolor RGBA PNG (color type 6), present in the spec since 1996. The name is export-dialog folklore.
  • "PNG-8 always looks bad." True only for photographic content. For flat graphics it is visually identical at a fraction of the size.
  • "PNG-8 can't have smooth transparency." Palette PNGs support 8-bit alpha per palette entry. With ≤256 shades needed on the edges, soft transparency works fine for most logos.
  • "Converting PNG-24 to PNG-8 is always safe." Always preview. Gradient-heavy images band; photos dither. The conversion adds no compression artefacts, but the palette reduction itself is a lossy step — and as the gradient row shows, it can even make the file bigger.

FAQ

Is PNG-32 an official format?

No. It is a colloquial name for PNG color type 6 (truecolor RGB plus 8-bit alpha). The PNG specification itself never uses the term; the label comes from export dialogs in 2000s design software.

Does PNG-8 support transparency?

Yes. Indexed PNGs can assign an alpha value, including full 8-bit alpha, to each palette entry. Hard-edged transparency always works; soft edges work as long as the anti-aliased edge shades fit within the palette.

Why is my PNG-8 bigger than my JPEG?

Different jobs. JPEG is a lossy photographic codec; PNG-8 is a lossless indexed format. For photographs, JPEG wins on size at equal visual quality — on our corpus, a 256-color PNG-8 of the test photograph was 3.6 times the size of a JPEG encoded from the same source at quality 85.

Can I convert back from PNG-8 to PNG-24 and recover the lost colors?

No. Palette reduction discards the original channel data. Converting upward only inflates the file; the colors are gone. Keep the original.

What palette size should I pick?

Start at 256, check the preview, then walk down (128, 64, 32) while the preview still looks clean. Flat logos often survive at 8 to 16 colors, which shrinks them further.

Do PNG-24 and PNG-32 look identical when there is no transparency?

Yes — pixel-identical. The 32-bit version just carries a redundant alpha channel. Export opaque images as PNG-24.
